Standardizing chaos: the AMAN Dashboard Revamp

How I transformed a B2B insurtech dashboard plagued by 6 different table styles, 4 competing detail page layouts, and zero design system into a unified, scalable product — without breaking what already worked.

Aman Design 2.0
B2B Insurtech · Indonesia

Aman is an insurtech start-up with the vision to supercharge & simplify employee health benefits experience in Indonesia, focusing on B2B product models of healthcare products as added value.

Overview

The dashboard that everyone used, and no one liked

Dashboards play a multitude of roles within our business, serving both our clients and internal teams. They're the daily operating surface for sales, claims, member management, and merchant operations — touched by dozens of people, every day.

However, the current state of our dashboard presented a real challenge. It was complex and disordered. Lacking consistency and standardization, it fell short of meeting our usability standards. This case study walks through how we overhauled it and established a uniform structure — enhancing both consistency and user-friendliness.

Background & Condition

Why now?

Two pressures collided at the same time — and that collision is what made this revamp finally happen.

Inconsistency

The revamp was triggered when the product design team was asked to build a new feature on the dashboard. We saw inconsistent components, flows, and interactions everywhere — with no documentation explaining why. We were genuinely confused about what patterns to follow for the new feature, which is itself a bad sign for any team.

Aman Design Rebranding

At the same time, Aman had introduced new branding across the wider product suite — refreshed logo, new primary color, updated typography, and a mascot. Unfortunately, none of this had been implemented in the dashboard yet. The two pressures together made it clear: this was the moment to revamp.

Case Gathering

Deep dive into the condition of our current dashboard

Before designing anything new, I started by collecting every dashboard screen — every menu, every flow, every form — and grouping them by similar issues. The goal wasn't to redesign on instinct, but to let the patterns of inconsistency surface on their own. Here's what we found.

01

Inconsistency of Tables

Dashboards live and die by tables — and ours had a lot of data poured into them. The problem? Six different table styles existed across the product, with no shared rules. Different headers, different row heights, different filter placements, different action button treatments, different empty states.

02

Inconsistency of Detail Pages

Many features had a "detail page" — the second-level view that shows the full record behind a table row. We found four different detail page layouts, each with its own approach to content hierarchy, component placement, navigation back to the list, and inline editing. Users essentially had to relearn the page every time they entered a different module.

03

Limited Space to Input/Edit Data

Some data fields require many characters — full names, company addresses, ICD-10 codes, currency amounts. But the dashboard supported inline editing directly inside table rows, where the available width was tiny. Users couldn't see what they were typing, couldn't tell which fields were required, and couldn't validate their input visually.

04

Unclear Conditions & Affordances

Two related problems lived under this label, both about users not knowing what they were looking at.

Navigation has no current state

The sidebar should tell the user where they are — which menu, which sub-menu. But the active state simply wasn't visible. Users opened the dashboard with no anchor point.

Icons that mean nothing

Bare icons like "+", "?", and undecorated chevrons appeared throughout the UI without labels or tooltips. Users guessed at what they did — and guessed wrong.

Standardization

Before designing, I went back into the dashboard

It was tempting to jump straight into Figma. But that would have just produced four prettier table styles instead of six ugly ones. Instead, I went back into the dashboard one more time — this time looking for the common content, common actions, and common flows that every screen shared underneath the visual chaos.

The standardization had two goals: make the dashboard consistent, but still leave room for genuine customization where the data demanded it. A claims table and an invoices table look similar — they don't have to be identical.

What standardization actually covered

01 · Tables

One table, infinite content

Header, table-head, table-body, footer — locked. Filters, search, column visibility, pagination — same place, every time.

02 · Detail Pages

Predictable second-level

Back button. Page title. Status pill. Tabbed left navigation. Content panel. Same skeleton, different content per module.

03 · Forms

Modals, not table cells

Add / Edit moved into proper modals with full-width inputs, required-field markers, and visible validation states.

04 · Navigation

You are here

Sidebar with explicit active state, sub-menu indentation, badge counts, and brand-aligned visual treatment.

05 · Iconography

Icon + label, always

No bare "+" buttons. Every action icon paired with a clear label or tooltip. Consistent stroke weight across the set.

06 · Brand

Aman Design 2.0

New logo, primary teal, refreshed typography, and the mascot — finally implemented in the dashboard, fully.

Process Recap

The five-step path from chaos to system

1

Audit every screen

Collected every dashboard menu, every flow, every form. Treated the existing UI as the research artifact rather than starting from a blank Figma file.

2

Group by issue, not by feature

Sorted screens by the type of inconsistency — table styles together, detail pages together, navigation issues together. The patterns surfaced themselves.

3

Map common content, action, and flow

Built a sitemap-style diagram of every recurring component: table head, table body, pagination, filter, search, batch actions, empty states. Same exercise for detail pages.

4

Standardize, with escape hatches

Locked the skeleton (header, body, footer regions, navigation patterns) but left module-level customization for genuinely different data needs. Consistency without rigidity.

5

Roll out branding alongside structure

Implemented Aman Design 2.0 — logo, primary teal, typography, mascot — together with the structural revamp, so users experienced the new dashboard as a single coherent change rather than two confusing ones.

Lessons Learned

What this project actually taught me

01

Audit before you redesign

The temptation when a UI feels broken is to open Figma. The real win came from going backwards first — collecting screens, grouping by issue, letting the patterns surface. The redesign was almost trivial once the audit was done.

02

Standardization ≠ uniformity

Locking the skeleton while leaving module-level customization meant the system held up for genuinely different data — claims, invoices, merchants, members — without forcing one shape over all of them. Rigid systems break the moment edge cases appear.

03

Branding alone won't fix structure

The new logo, color, and mascot were ready — but on top of an inconsistent dashboard they would have just been lipstick. Pairing the brand rollout with structural standardization is what made the whole thing feel new.

04

Inconsistency is a leadership signal

Six table styles don't appear because designers can't draw tables. They appear because no one owns the system. The most valuable output of this project wasn't the new dashboard — it was the shared language the team now uses to discuss what "consistent" actually means.
