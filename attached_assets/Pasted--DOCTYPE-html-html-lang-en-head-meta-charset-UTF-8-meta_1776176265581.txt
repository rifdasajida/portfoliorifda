    <!-- ── Overview ── -->
    <section class="block reveal">
      <h2>Overview</h2>
      <p>As someone on a Quran memorization journey, I struggled daily with a problem no existing app solved well. So I decided to design and build a tool that fixed it for <em>me</em> — then kept iterating until it worked for my community, too. This case study documents that end-to-end journey: the problem, the pivots, the technical hurdles, and the lessons.</p>
    </section>

    <!-- ── TL;DR ── -->
    <section class="block reveal">
      <div class="tldr">
        <div class="tldr-lbl">TL;DR</div>
        <div class="tldr-row">
          <div class="tldr-k">Problem</div>
          <div class="tldr-v">Existing Quran apps offer full-surah audio and passive reading — but none handle the real murojaah workflow: verse-level repetition, self-validated recitation, and cross-surah doubt.</div>
        </div>
        <div class="tldr-row">
          <div class="tldr-k">Solution</div>
          <div class="tldr-v">Mobile-first web app with speech-recognized recitation validation, verse-level audio looping, per-ayah progress tracking, and a "Sambung Ayat" practice mode — no install, no signup wall.</div>
        </div>
        <div class="tldr-row">
          <div class="tldr-k">My role</div>
          <div class="tldr-v">Everything. Problem framing, UX, frontend, speech matcher engine, database schema, RLS security, OAuth, deployment.</div>
        </div>
        <div class="tldr-row">
          <div class="tldr-k">Biggest win</div>
          <div class="tldr-v">Shipped a production app end-to-end as a designer. Learned that product thinking and technical execution are the <em>same</em> discipline when you can't hide behind a team.</div>
        </div>
      </div>
    </section>

    <!-- ── The Problem ── -->
    <section class="block reveal">
      <h2>The Problem</h2>
      <p>A few months ago, I joined a murojaah community — a small group of friends committed to staying consistent with our Quran memorization. Every week, we'd take turns reciting from memory, testing each other, holding each other accountable. And every week, I ran into the same two problems.</p>

      <div class="problems">
        <div class="problem">
          <div>
            <h3>The loop of doubt</h3>
            <p>I'd close the Mushaf and begin to recite. Two ayahs in, a thought: <em>"Wait — was that verse correct?"</em> I'd reopen the Mushaf, check, close it again, continue. Two lines later, the doubt returned. Sometimes I'd unconsciously slip into a different surah because verse endings sounded alike — suddenly I was lost, focus broken.</p>
          </div>
        </div>
        <div class="problem">
          <div>
            <h3>No verse-level repetition</h3>
            <p>I memorize best when I can loop a specific verse while listening — over and over, until the rhythm is in my bones. But every audio app I tried only offered full-surah playback. I'd manually pause, rewind, guess the timestamp. The tool was fighting my workflow instead of supporting it.</p>
          </div>
        </div>
      </div>

      <div class="callout">
        <h3>Why not just download another app?</h3>
        <p>I looked. I tried browser-based players, native apps, YouTube playlists. Nothing clicked. And downloading yet another app wasn't an option — my phone storage has been permanently fighting for survival at 99% for months. That's when I thought: <em>"What if I just build it myself?"</em></p>
      </div>
    </section>

    <!-- ── Process ── -->
    <section class="block reveal">
      <h2>Process</h2>
      <p>Rather than jumping to Figma, I used a builder-first approach: validate the hardest technical unknown first, then iterate design based on real user testing.</p>

      <div class="steps">
        <div class="step">
          <div class="step-n">1</div>
          <div class="step-body">
            <h3>Problem validation & competitive audit</h3>
            <p>Spent a weekend auditing every major Quran app I could find — free, paid, local, international. Looked specifically for verse-level looping, real-time recitation validation, and per-ayah progress tracking. Most apps optimized for <em>reading</em>, not for practicing recitation from memory. The gap was real.</p>
          </div>
        </div>
        <div class="step">
          <div class="step-n">2</div>
          <div class="step-body">
            <h3>Iteration 01 — builder mode (ugly on purpose)</h3>
            <p>Ignored UX deliberately. Built a rough working version to de-risk the biggest unknown: <em>could the browser's speech recognition API actually understand Arabic recitation well enough to validate memorization?</em> Once it (barely) worked, I shared it with my murojaah friends to test.</p>
          </div>
        </div>
        <div class="step">
          <div class="step-n">3</div>
          <div class="step-body">
            <h3>Reality check — user testing</h3>
            <p>What made sense to me did not make sense to anyone else. Friends got confused by the flow, tapped wrong buttons, missed features I thought were obvious. The feedback that stung most: <em>"It works, but I don't understand what to do next."</em> Five words that reset my approach entirely.</p>
          </div>
        </div>
        <div class="step">
          <div class="step-n">4</div>
          <div class="step-body">
            <h3>Iteration 02 — designer pivot</h3>
            <p>Key observation: 100% of my friends opened the app on their phones. I was the only desktop user. I rebuilt top-down with three rules — mobile-first (not mobile-compatible), Indonesian-consistent, and no feature without a verb. Every screen had to answer "what do I do next?" within one glance.</p>
          </div>
        </div>
        <div class="step">
          <div class="step-n">5</div>
          <div class="step-body">
            <h3>Technical hardening & shipping</h3>
            <p>Added Row-Level Security in Supabase, XSS escaping, defense-in-depth user_id filters, FK cascade for compliance, Google OAuth with custom branding. All invisible to users, all non-negotiable. Deployed to Vercel with a custom domain and tested across mobile browsers before a soft launch to the community.</p>
          </div>
        </div>
      </div>
    </section>

    <!-- ── Product Decisions ── -->
    <section class="block reveal">
      <h2>Key Product Decisions</h2>
      <p>Five forks in the road that shaped the app. Each was a trade-off I had to make alone, without a PM to argue with.</p>

      <div class="decisions">
        <div class="decision">
          <div class="decision-q">Guest mode or force login?</div>
          <div class="decision-a"><strong>Guest, local-only.</strong> Login-first walls kill first impressions. I let guest users store progress in localStorage and sync to Supabase only after sign-in. This mirrors how I wanted to try the app myself — try first, commit later.</div>
        </div>
        <div class="decision">
          <div class="decision-q">Tab sync or tab independent?</div>
          <div class="decision-a"><strong>Tab sync (industry standard).</strong> If a user logs out in one tab, every tab logs out. Forcing per-tab login is worse than the alternative — and matches how Gmail, Notion, and every serious web app behave.</div>
        </div>
        <div class="decision">
          <div class="decision-q">Speech recognition or manual input?</div>
          <div class="decision-a"><strong>Speech, despite the complexity.</strong> Manual input would've been 10× easier but 10× less useful. Murojaah <em>is</em> reciting out loud. Typing misses the entire point of the exercise.</div>
        </div>
        <div class="decision">
          <div class="decision-q">Per-surah or per-juz picker?</div>
          <div class="decision-a"><strong>Both, via segmented control.</strong> Different users think in different units. Exam-prep friends wanted "test me on Juz 30". I wanted "test me on Al-Ma'arij". Segmented tabs respect both mental models.</div>
        </div>
        <div class="decision">
          <div class="decision-q">Hide technical errors or surface them?</div>
          <div class="decision-a"><strong>Surface them in Indonesian with a suggested action.</strong> "Mikrofon belum aktif. Klik ikon kunci di address bar." Users fail gracefully only if they understand what failed. Silent failures are the worst possible UX.</div>
        </div>
      </div>
    </section>

    <!-- ── Technical Deep Dive ── -->
    <section class="block reveal">
      <h2>Technical Deep Dive</h2>
      <p>The hardest part wasn't the design — it was teaching a browser to understand a recitation in a language it wasn't optimized for.</p>

      <h3>Speech recognition, and why it broke my brain</h3>
      <p>The Web Speech API is the browser's built-in speech-to-text engine. Free. Works. And wildly inconsistent — especially for Arabic. I spent more time on this single feature than on every other feature combined.</p>
      <ul>
        <li>Same recitation produced different results on Chrome desktop vs Chrome Android vs Safari iOS.</li>
        <li>Microphone permissions behaved differently — granted silently, required user gestures, required HTTPS, revoked between sessions.</li>
        <li>Some browsers handled Arabic pronunciation better than others (Chrome best; Safari a gamble).</li>
        <li>Android Chrome plays an audible "ding" at recognition start — impossible to disable at app level.</li>
      </ul>

      <h3>When "fifty thousand" broke everything</h3>
      <p>Early on I tested Al-Ma'arij verse 4, which contains the number <strong>50,000 years</strong>. The browser transcribed my recitation as <code>"خمسين ألف"</code> (phonetic Arabic), but my reference text had <code>"50,000"</code> as digits. My matcher compared them character-by-character, decided they didn't match, and marked a perfectly correct recitation as wrong.</p>

      <pre class="code"><span class="c">// Map digit sequences to their Arabic spoken equivalents</span>
<span class="k">const</span> <span class="f">_NUM_TO_AR</span> = {
  <span class="s">'50000'</span>: <span class="s">'خمسين الف'</span>,
  <span class="s">'70000'</span>: <span class="s">'سبعين الف'</span>,
  <span class="s">'50'</span>: <span class="s">'خمسين'</span>, <span class="s">'70'</span>: <span class="s">'سبعين'</span>,
  <span class="s">'19'</span>: <span class="s">'تسعة عشر'</span>, <span class="s">'12'</span>: <span class="s">'اثنا عشر'</span>,
  <span class="c">// ...and so on</span>
};

<span class="k">function</span> <span class="f">_digitsToArabic</span>(text) {
  <span class="k">return</span> text.replace(<span class="s">/\d+/g</span>, m =&gt; _NUM_TO_AR[m] || m);
}</pre>

      <h3>Lookahead & gap tolerance</h3>
      <p>Reciters don't always mispronounce — sometimes they <em>skip</em> a word, or pause long enough that the API interprets it as silence. A strictly sequential matcher would cascade errors from one missed word. The fix was a bounded lookahead.</p>

      <div class="callout success">
        <h3>Matcher behavior</h3>
        <p>Before marking a word wrong, look ahead up to <strong>3 words</strong> in the expected sequence. If a match exists within that window, treat skipped words as neutral <code>'passed'</code>. For gaps of 2+ words, require a stricter similarity threshold (<code>MATCH_THRESHOLD + 0.08</code>) to prevent the matcher from accidentally claiming a wildly different ayah as a match.</p>
      </div>

      <p>This single change turned the matcher from "frustrating and punishing" into "forgiving but honest" — exactly the feedback loop a person memorizing the Quran needs.</p>

      <h3>Security is a design decision</h3>
      <p>When you're a solo builder launching a personal tool, security feels like the thing you do "later". It wasn't.</p>
      <ul>
        <li><strong>Row-Level Security</strong> on every Supabase table, targeting the <code>authenticated</code> role with correct <code>USING</code> + <code>WITH CHECK</code> clauses.</li>
        <li><strong>Storage policies</strong> ensuring users could only upload to folders named with their own <code>auth.uid()</code>.</li>
        <li><strong>FK <code>ON DELETE CASCADE</code></strong> so deleting a user also deletes all their setoran — compliance with GDPR-style data rights.</li>
        <li><strong>XSS escaping</strong> in every modal rendering user-supplied data, plus audio URL scheme validation.</li>
        <li><strong>Defense-in-depth filters</strong> at the query level, even with RLS enabled — because one day I might misconfigure RLS, and the code layer would still save me.</li>
      </ul>
    </section>

    <section class="block reveal">
      <h2>Lessons Learned</h2>
      <p>The bugs I fixed are on GitHub. The lessons I learned are here.</p>

      <div class="lessons">
        <div class="lesson">
          <div class="lesson-n">01</div>
          <div class="lesson-body">
            <h3>Personal itch ≠ product success</h3>
            <p>I built v1 for myself and it worked — for me. The moment anyone else touched it, every assumption became a friction point. A personal tool becomes a product the moment a second human uses it, and the work between those two states is the real design work.</p>
          </div>
        </div>
        <div class="lesson">
          <div class="lesson-n">02</div>
          <div class="lesson-body">
            <h3>UX intuition is a starting point, not a shortcut</h3>
            <p>Years of design experience didn't stop me from shipping a v1 that confused testers in 30 seconds. Intuition points you in a direction. Testing tells you if the direction was correct. Skipping the second step is how designers ship bad products.</p>
          </div>
        </div>
        <div class="lesson">
          <div class="lesson-n">03</div>
          <div class="lesson-body">
            <h3>Technical constraints are design material</h3>
            <p>The speech API's quirks forced me to design around uncertainty — retry modes, forgiving matchers, visible confidence indicators. Constraints didn't limit the design; they shaped it into something more honest.</p>
          </div>
        </div>
        <div class="lesson">
          <div class="lesson-n">04</div>
          <div class="lesson-body">
            <h3>Security is a trust decision</h3>
            <p>RLS, XSS escaping, data isolation — these sound like backend concerns. They're not. They determine whether a user feels safe trusting your app with their recitation recordings. I stopped thinking of security as "the dev's job" and started thinking of it as <em>trust design</em>.</p>
          </div>
        </div>
        <div class="lesson">
          <div class="lesson-n">05</div>
          <div class="lesson-body">
            <h3>Shipping is a skill, not an event</h3>
            <p>Before this project, shipping felt like a moment — the day you "go live". Now I understand shipping as a muscle: cutting scope, holding quality lines, saying "this version is good enough to show someone" without flinching. I'm still training that muscle.</p>
          </div>
        </div>
        <div class="lesson">
          <div class="lesson-n">06</div>
          <div class="lesson-body">
            <h3>Belajar sambil blangsakan</h3>
            <p>An Indonesian expression that loosely translates to "learning while making a mess of it". My first GitHub push. My first Supabase RLS policy. My first OAuth consent screen. Every single one was a mess. Every single one taught me something I now know forever.</p>
          </div>
        </div>
      </div>
    </section>
